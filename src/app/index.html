Janus, the Mermin-Peres square on two qubits

One tile, two true colors

Every square here has two true colors. Read it along its row and it shows one color. Read it down its column and it can show another. At least one square always changes, and the physics guarantees it.

For exampleYour website's Buy button uses one of these squares. One visitor sees it teal, the next sees it coral, and both pages still follow all six color rules, so the design never breaks.

Goal: Find a world where only one square changes.

The science, for the curious

Each tile is a quantum observable. Read it with its row or with its column and it can take a different color, and no fixed coloring could ever satisfy all six rules at once.

Mermin's Dare

Try to color it classically

A puzzle with no solution. Put a plus or a minus on each square so every row and column multiplies out to the sign it asks for. You will always break at least one rule.

For exampleIt's like a sudoku that can't be finished. The best anyone can do is 5 of the 6 rules. A quantum computer gets all 6, every time, because its squares don't carry fixed answers.

Goal: Get 5 of 6 rules by flipping squares yourself.

The science, for the curious

Give every tile a fixed value, +1 or −1. Every row must multiply to +1, columns one and two to +1, and column three to −1. Tap tiles to flip them.

-

Why it fails

Multiply all nine values row by row and you get +1. Multiply them column by column and you get −1. Same nine numbers, two answers, so one rule always breaks.

Quantum measurement escapes this because a tile has no value until it is read, and the value it gets depends on its partners. That is contextuality, and Qontext turns it into color.

Checked by computer

All 512 fixed assignments were searched. The best any of them can do is 5 of 6 rules.

Fringe, a Mach-Zehnder interferometer for color

Mix colors like waves

Mix two colors the way waves of light mix. Turn the dial and color slides from one output to the other. Nothing is lost: what leaves one side shows up on the other.

For examplePut a color and its opposite in, like blue and orange. At the right turn of the dial one output fades to plain gray while the other glows twice as strong.

Goal: Turn the dial until one output fades to gray.

The science, for the curious

Each color becomes a wave: hue is its phase, chroma its amplitude. Two colors meet at a beam splitter and leave through two ports. Shift the phase and the ports trade color while the total stays constant.

Pentacle, the KCBS test on one qutrit

The pentagram palette

Five colors on a star. Colors joined by a line can never both be picked, and the size of each dot is its chance. Ordinary rules cap the total chance at 2. Quantum rules go higher.

For exampleUse it for five chart categories. Colors joined on the star sit far apart on the color wheel, so no two connected slices ever look alike.

Goal: Turn the knobs back to the top score, 2.236.

The science, for the curious

Five quantum events where neighbours on the star can never both happen. Any classical model keeps their total chance at 2 or below. The right qutrit state reaches the square root of 5.

Spooky Pair, a Bell pair and the CHSH game

Entangled assets

Two designs, one light and one dark, share a linked pair of quantum coins. Each design picks a style and flips its coin, and the two coins land in step more often than any two separate coins could.

For exampleA light landing page and its dark twin. When both are calm their accent colors tend to match. When both are bold they tend to clash. Two separate dice can't keep that promise this well.

Goal: Set both to Bold and catch them clashing.

Unpaintables, Kochen-Specker shape packs

Shapes no coloring can finish

3D shapes made of arrows. The rule sounds easy: in every group of arrows at right angles, light up exactly one. For two of these shapes nobody, and no computer, can follow that rule everywhere. Something always breaks.

For exampleExport one as an SVG or a 3D file and you have a logo or a small sculpture that is mathematically impossible to color in, with the broken spot showing every time.

Goal: Find the one shape that can be colored.

The science, for the curious

Each shape is a set of directions in space. A classical coloring must light exactly one direction in every perpendicular set and never light two perpendicular directions. For Peres 33 and Cabello 18 that is impossible, and the broken set always shows.

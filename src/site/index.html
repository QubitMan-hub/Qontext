Tiles you touch collapse

Albers proved color depends on its neighbours. Quantum physics proves value does too.

In 1963 Josef Albers made one color look like two by changing what surrounded it. That is perception. Qontext goes further: here a color genuinely is different depending on its context, and the mathematics says no fixed coloring could ever match it.

Pair Production

A sneaker drop, start to finish

A pair of shoes is two contexts. The left foot reads your grid by its rows and the right foot by its columns, so one panel can be two true colors, one on each foot. Design a pair the way a sneaker artist would, then paint it yourself.

  1. 1Roll your world

    Every visitor gets their own world: one measurement of the grid, rolled the moment the page opened. That world decides which panels can change between the feet. Roll a new one and the whole site changes with it.

    World -

  2. 2Tune the harmony

    A sneaker artist starts from a palette. Here the palette is a harmony: colors spaced evenly round the wheel, like the phases of a quantum system. Turn it to move every color at once, and pick how many colors share the shoe.

    • 3Bouncer checks the logo

      The logo sits on the stripe, and the stripe can come out in two colors. Bouncer picks the ink that reads on both and holds it to 4.5 to 1, the bar for readable text. Slide the paint toward the middle tones and watch it fail. Fix finds the nearest lightness that reads.

      Logo ink on the weaker stripe color -

    • 4Paint the pair

      Tap a panel to measure it. It lands on its row color on the left foot and its column color on the right. Most panels match. A few cannot, and the maths says no paint job could make them all match and still follow every rule.

        The six rules, in shoe terms. Every panel has a home color and a flipped one, its opposite on the wheel.

        • 5The drop

          A drop is a limited run, and here every pair in it is a different world. Twelve of them, each one following all six rules. Pick one to make it yours.

          • 6The tag

            Every pair ships with a tag that says exactly which world it came from. The fingerprint is a SHA-256 of the design, so anyone can check the pair is the one you made.

          Janus

          Mermin-Peres square, 1990

          Nine tiles, each a quantum observable. Read a tile along its row and it takes one color; read it down its column and it can take another, and both readings keep their rules. That lets a brand system change with the page, the season or the partner and still hold together.

          Mermin's Dare

          -

          Rows must multiply to +1. Columns one and two to +1. Column three to −1.

          After N. David Mermin, 1990

          Give each tile a fixed value, +1 or −1, and try to satisfy all six rules. Five is the best anyone gets; a computer checked all 512 ways. Quantum measurement gets six every time, which is why a Qontext palette can't be faked.

          Unity Wheel

          Roots of unity

          A system with d quantum levels has d phases spread evenly around a circle. On a color wheel those are the classic harmonies: two levels give a complementary pair, three a triad, four a square. Scroll, and the whole harmony turns at once.

          Fringe

          After Mach and Zehnder, 1892

          Each color becomes a wave: hue is its phase, chroma its height. Two colors meet at a beam splitter and leave by two ports. Drag across the band to shift the phase, and the ports trade color while their total stays exactly the same. It gives light and dark gradients that always balance.

          Pentacle

          Klyachko, Can, Binicioğlu and Shumovsky, 2008

          Five events on a star, where linked events exclude each other. Any classical model keeps their total chance at 2 or less. One qutrit reaches the square root of 5, and the palette follows that geometry: five colors for charts and maps where neighbours never look alike.

          Spooky Pair

          Clauser, Horne, Shimony and Holt, 1969

          The light half and the dark half of this wall share one entangled pair. Each side picks a style, and its accent is the measurement outcome. Over many pairs they agree more often than any two independent rules could, so light and dark themes stay linked without anyone matching them by hand.

          Unpaintables

          After Kochen and Specker, 1967

          Each shape is a set of directions in space. A classical coloring has to light exactly one direction in every perpendicular set, and never two perpendicular ones. For Peres 33 and Cabello 18 that can't be done, so one set always breaks, and it glows. The Atelier exports them as SVG and OBJ for prints and sculpture.

          Notary

          SHA-256 seal on IBM Quantum shots

          Run the circuits on IBM Quantum with Probe and load the results here. Notary scores them against the classical limits and seals the shots with a SHA-256 hash, the device name and the job record, so a print edition carries provenance anyone can check.

          This seal comes from the example pool that ships with Qontext, measured on a noisy simulator, and it says so. Seals from IBM hardware name the device.
